1. Purpose Statement


This Brand Positioning & Messaging Guide exists to give every team at
AlfredCo.Host -- marketing, sales, product, support, partnerships, and
PR -- a single, authoritative source for how Alfred is positioned, described, and differentiated in market.


The guide is:


- Strategically defensible. Every positioning choice traces back to validated buyer needs, canonical value propositions, and competitive reality -- not aspiration or assumption.


- Evidence-led. Claims are either backed by verifiable data or accompanied by an evidence plan that defines what data is needed, how it will be collected, and by when.


- Operationally credible. The messaging system is built to be used, not admired. Templates, battlecards, channel playbooks, and persona maps translate strategy into daily execution.


- Consistent across time and teams. Terminology, tone, and positioning decisions are documented and versioned. Anyone joining the team six months from now should be able to produce on-brand content using this guide alone.


- Boldly differentiated. Alfred is an autonomous system, not a chatbot.
The guide leads with this truth confidently and consistently.


What this guide is NOT: a visual brand identity manual (no logos, colors, or typography), a media buying strategy, a product roadmap, or a pricing strategy document.

2.1 Category Definition


Primary Category


Autonomous AI Receptionist for Short-Term Rentals


Alfred does not compete in the "chatbot" category or the "guest messaging tool" category. Alfred occupies a category above both: an autonomous system that handles 100% of guest interactions across the entire stay journey, escalating to the host only when physical intervention is required.


This category choice is deliberate. The market currently segments into three tiers:


Tier 1 -- Template tools and basic chatbots: Scheduled messages, saved replies, simple FAQ bots. Require constant human oversight. Automation coverage: 30-50% at best. (Examples of the pattern: HostBuddy-class tools, basic PMS messaging features.)


Tier 2 -- AI-assisted messaging with draft review: AI suggests responses; humans review and approve. Reduces time but does not eliminate the work. Automation coverage: 50-70%. (Examples of the pattern: Besty AI-class tools, early-generation PMS AI features.)


Tier 3 -- Autonomous AI systems: AI reads, decides, responds, and escalates independently. Humans intervene only for physical tasks or edge cases. Automation coverage: 70-100%. (Alfred sits here, at the top end.)


Alfred's positioning claim: we operate at the ceiling of Tier 3. Where even the strongest competitors report 70-90% autonomous handling, Alfred handles 100% of digital interactions. The host's phone rings only when a guest needs something physical that no AI can provide.


Secondary Category


Guest Communication Automation Platform -- used when buyers search broadly and have not yet defined their need precisely. This secondary framing captures search-stage buyers who don't yet know they need full autonomy.


SEO Validation of Category Choice
The TRAFFIC Intelligence keyword analysis (March 2026, 223 keywords, 103,000 monthly searches) validates the primary category choice with quantitative demand data:
"AI receptionist" commands 3,600 monthly searches at KD 22, making it both high-demand and achievable. Critically, 100% of current SERP results are phone/voice AI receptionists for dental, medical, and legal verticals. Zero hospitality results exist. This means AlfredCo has a first-mover opportunity to claim and define this term for hospitality before competitors recognize the gap. The combined AI receptionist/concierge cluster (including "concierge ai" at 720/mo KD 0, "ai front desk" at 390/mo KD 19, "auto receptionist" at 320/mo KD 19, and "ai concierge" at 260/mo KD 0) totals approximately 5,300 monthly searches.
The secondary category ("Guest Communication Automation Platform") is also validated by emerging search demand. "Guest messaging software" (+600% 3-month trend), "hotel guest messaging" (+600%), and "guest messaging platform" (+600%) are category-defining queries that barely existed 6 months ago. This signals the market is actively creating the category AlfredCo's secondary positioning occupies.

2.5 Anti-Positioning: What Alfred Explicitly Is NOT

Alfred is NOT a messaging template tool. Templates are static. Alfred generates contextual, personalized responses in real-time based on property data, guest history, and conversation context.


Alfred is NOT a PMS feature. Alfred is an independent platform that integrates with any PMS. It is not locked inside someone else's ecosystem and does not depend on a specific PMS for its intelligence.


Alfred is NOT a lead generation tool. Alfred serves the host's guests, not Alfred's business development. It does not inject its own branding into guest conversations or harvest guest data for third-party purposes.


Alfred is NOT a guest-facing brand. Guests interact with Alfred as if they are communicating with the host. Alfred is invisible infrastructure, not a consumer brand competing for the guest's attention.


Alfred is NOT "AI-assisted." AI-assisted means a human still makes the final call. Alfred is AI-autonomous. The human is a safety net for physical intervention, not a required step in the communication chain.

  5.1 Competitive Philosophy


Alfred's Competitive Stance


Per D-002 and D-003: Bold authority with category contrast. Alfred does not react to competitors -- Alfred defines its own category and lets competitors react to us.


Core principles:


1. Lead with what Alfred does, not what competitors don't. Every competitive conversation opens with Alfred's capability, then contextualizes the gap.


2. Contrast categories, never name companies. Public-facing materials use category labels (Ch07 Section 7.3): "cheap chatbots," "template-based tools," "draft-and-review platforms," "generic AI assistants."


3. Competitor names are INTERNAL ONLY. Battlecards containing competitor names are marked clearly and restricted to sales enablement use.


4. Claims about competitors use their own published data. Alfred never makes unverified claims about what competitors can or cannot do. All competitor capability claims are sourced from their own websites, documentation, or third-party comparison guides and logged in the ResearchLog.


5. The conversation is always about the host. Competitive messaging serves the host's decision-making, not Alfred's ego. Frame every comparison as: "Here is what matters for your operation, and here is how each approach addresses it."

Chapter 06 -- Voice & Tone
Version: 1.0 | Date: 2026-02-19 | Status: APPROVED (post-audit release)


PURPOSE


This chapter defines how Alfred speaks -- the consistent personality traits that form Alfred's brand voice, and the contextual tone shifts that adapt delivery to audience, channel, and situation. Everything here flows from D-004 (calm authority) and the emotional core established in Ch01 and Ch03.


Voice is constant. Tone flexes. This chapter codifies both.


Governance: All public-facing copy, UI text, support responses, and marketing materials must align with this chapter. Deviations require Brand Strategist approval and a logged exception in the DecisionRegistry.


6.1 BRAND VOICE DEFINITION


Alfred's voice is the voice of calm, competent control. It sounds like the operations expert you hired who already handled everything before you thought to ask.


One-line voice statement: Alfred speaks with the quiet confidence of someone who has already solved the problem.


6.2 VOICE PILLARS


Four pillars define Alfred's voice. Every piece of copy should reflect at least two.


Pillar 1: Calm Authority
Alfred knows what it is doing. No hedging, no over-explaining, no apologies for capability. Statements are direct and declarative. This is not arrogance -- it is earned confidence backed by product fact.
- Sounds like: "Alfred handles 100% of guest conversations autonomously."
- Does not sound like: "Alfred can try to help with some of your guest messages!"


Pillar 2: Operations-First Clarity
Alfred speaks in outcomes, not features. Every statement answers "so what?" for the host. Technical capability is translated into operational benefit. Jargon is eliminated unless the audience expects it.
- Sounds like: "Your guests get instant answers. You get uninterrupted sleep."
- Does not sound like: "Our NLP engine processes multi-turn conversational threads."


Pillar 3: Relief-Forward Empathy
Alfred acknowledges the host's pain without dwelling on it. The emotional arc moves quickly from "we understand the problem" to "here is how it disappears." No fear-based marketing. No guilt. No manufactured urgency.
- Sounds like: "No more 2 AM check-in questions. Alfred is already on it."
- Does not sound like: "Are you STILL answering guest messages manually?! You're losing bookings!"


Pillar 4: Professional Warmth
Alfred is approachable but never casual to the point of undermining credibility. The tone sits between "trusted business partner" and "competent colleague" -- never "enthusiastic startup bro" and never "cold enterprise vendor."
- Sounds like: "Let's get your listings covered. It takes about ten minutes."
- Does not sound like: "OMG you're gonna LOVE this!! Let's gooo!"

6.4 TONE FLEXES BY CONTEXT


Voice stays constant. Tone shifts to match the context. The six primary contexts below define how Alfred's voice adapts without losing its core identity.


Marketing: Most confident, benefit-forward. Strong claims, calm delivery. Lead with outcomes. This is where Alfred's authority is at its peak -- every sentence earns the host's attention.


Onboarding: Warmest, most supportive. Encourage action without patronizing. Micro-wins language ("Done. Your listing is connected."). Brevity is kindness -- hosts in setup mode want progress, not education.


Product UI: Most concise, operational. Zero fluff. Every word earns its place. Confirmations are one line. Errors include exactly one next step. Labels describe function, not personality.


Support: Most empathetic, patient. Solve first, explain second. Acknowledge friction without blame. Never assume user error -- frame issues as situations to resolve together.


Sales: Most authoritative, data-driven. Category contrast activated. Objection handling is direct but never combative. Comparisons reference categories (not named competitors). ROI framing leads.


Crisis: Most transparent, factual. Skip marketing language entirely. Lead with what happened, what is being done, and when the next update arrives. No hedging, no spin. Honesty is the fastest path to retained trust.

6.4b BEFORE/AFTER REWRITE EXAMPLES


These examples show common voice problems and how to fix them. Use these as calibration references for content review.


Rewrite 1: Feature Dumping --> Outcome-First


Before (fails Pillar 2):
"Alfred uses advanced natural language processing, multi-turn conversation management, and context-aware retrieval-augmented generation to process guest inquiries across all major OTA platforms including Airbnb, VRBO, and Booking.com."


After:
"Your guests get accurate, instant answers on every platform -- Airbnb, VRBO, Booking.com. Alfred reads the conversation, understands the question, and responds with full context about your property."


What changed: Technical jargon replaced with operational outcomes. The host does not care about NLP or RAG -- they care that guests get good answers fast.


Rewrite 2: Hype Language --> Calm Authority


Before (fails Pillar 1   Anti-pattern):
"Alfred is revolutionizing the hospitality industry with cutting-edge AI technology that will transform how you interact with guests forever!"


After:
"Alfred handles guest communication so you do not have to. Every question answered. Every check-in confirmed. Every review request sent -- autonomously."


What changed: "Revolutionizing" and "cutting-edge" are startup hype (Anti-pattern). "Forever" is unprovable. The rewrite states what Alfred does in concrete terms with calm confidence.


Rewrite 3: Fear-Based Urgency --> Relief-Forward


Before (fails Pillar 3):
"Every minute you waste answering guest messages is a minute you will never get back. Your competitors are already using AI. Can you afford to fall behind?"


After:
"Guest messages do not stop. But your involvement can. Alfred handles every conversation, so your time goes back to running your business -- or just living your life."


What changed: Fear framing ("waste," "never get back," "fall behind") replaced with relief framing. The pain is acknowledged ("messages do not stop") but the resolution is immediate and positive.


Rewrite 4: Over-Hedging --> Earned Confidence


Before (fails Pillar 1):
"Alfred might be able to help with some of your guest communication needs. We think it could potentially save you some time, though results may vary depending on your situation."


After:
"Alfred handles 100% of guest conversations autonomously. You decide what to oversee. Setup takes ten minutes."


What changed: "Might," "potentially," "some," "could" are hedging language that undermines credibility. Alfred's capabilities are product facts, not hopes. State them directly.


Rewrite 5: Casual Overshoot --> Professional Warmth


Before (fails Pillar 4):
"Hey there!! Super excited to have you on board! You're gonna absolutely crush it with Alfred -- let's get this party started! :D"


After:
"Welcome to Alfred. Let's get your listings connected. In about ten minutes, your guests will start getting instant responses -- and you will not need to send a single one."


What changed: Excessive enthusiasm undermines the "trusted operations expert" voice. Professional warmth means approachable without being performative. The rewrite is warm (welcome, collaborative "let's") without being casual.


6.4c CRISIS COMMUNICATION GUIDELINES


When things go wrong -- system outages, data incidents, service disruptions -- Alfred's voice shifts to its most transparent and factual register. Crisis communication follows three principles: honesty about the situation, clarity about the response, and commitment to follow-up.


Crisis Voice Principles


1. Lead with facts, not feelings. State what happened in plain language. Do not minimize, speculate, or euphemize. "Alfred experienced a service disruption" is better than "We had a tiny hiccup."


2. Own the impact. Acknowledge how the issue affects hosts and their guests. "During this period, guest messages were not being responded to automatically" is honest. "There may have been a slight delay in some responses" is evasive.


3. State the response and timeline. What is being done, and when will the next update arrive? Hosts need a timeline, even if approximate. "We are investigating and will update you within 2 hours" is actionable. "We are working on it" is not.


4. Skip all marketing language. No benefit claims, no brand positioning, no product superlatives. The only goal is restoring trust through transparency.


5. Follow up when promised. If you said 2 hours, update in 2 hours -- even if the update is "still investigating, next update in 2 more hours." Missed follow-ups destroy trust faster than the original incident.


Crisis Scenario Templates


Scenario 1: Service Outage (guest responses delayed or stopped)


Subject: "Alfred Service Update -- [Date]"


"We are writing to let you know that Alfred experienced a service disruption starting at [time] [timezone]. During this period, automatic guest responses were delayed [or: were not being sent].


Our engineering team identified the issue at [time] and [has resolved it / is actively working on a fix]. [If resolved: Normal service resumed at [time]. All pending guest messages have been processed.] [If ongoing: We expect service to be restored within [timeframe]. We will send another update by [specific time].]


We understand that reliable guest communication is why you use Alfred. We take this seriously and are [implementing monitoring to prevent recurrence / conducting a full review of what happened].


If any of your guests were affected and you need assistance, reply to this email and our team will help directly."


Scenario 2: Data or Privacy Concern


Subject: "Important Security Update from Alfred"


"We are contacting you about a security matter that may affect your account. [Describe what happened in factual terms -- e.g., 'On [date], we identified unauthorized access to [specific system]. No guest personal data or payment information was accessed.']


What we know: [Factual summary of scope, what data was/was not affected]


What we have done: [Specific remediation steps taken]


What you should do: [Any required user actions -- e.g., password reset, review of settings]


We will provide a full incident report by [date]. If you have questions before then, contact our security team at [email]."


Scenario 3: Feature Degradation (partial service impact)


"Quick update: [Feature name] is currently operating with reduced performance. Guest responses are still being sent, but [specific impact -- e.g., response times are longer than usual / local recommendation answers may be less detailed].


We identified the cause and expect full performance to be restored by [time/date]. No action is needed on your end.


We will confirm once everything is back to normal."

6.4d AI-GENERATED PRODUCT COPY GUIDELINES


Alfred's product generates messages that guests read as if they came from the host. This means Alfred's in-product voice has a dual responsibility: it must feel like a competent, friendly host to the guest, and it must remain within the brand's voice boundaries for the host who configured it.


Principles for AI-Generated Guest-Facing Copy


1. Hospitality register, not brand register. When Alfred talks to guests, it does not sound like a marketing brand. It sounds like a knowledgeable, attentive host. The guest should never feel they are talking to a company -- they should feel they are getting a responsive, helpful property host.


2. Property-specific, not generic. Alfred's responses should reflect the specific property's details, house rules, and local context. Generic responses ("Thank you for your message!") fail the hospitality standard. Specific responses ("Check-in is at 3 PM. The lockbox code will be sent 24 hours before arrival. Street parking is free on your block.") build guest confidence.


3. Warm but efficient. Guest-facing copy balances friendliness with brevity. Guests want answers, not essays. A good Alfred guest response is 2-4 sentences for a standard question, longer only when the question requires detailed instructions (e.g., complex check-in procedures).


4. Never reveal the AI. Alfred does not identify itself as AI to guests unless the host has explicitly configured transparency mode. No "As an AI assistant..." preambles. No hedging that hints at non-human authorship. The guest interacts with what feels like the host's own communication.


5. Escalation language is calm, not alarming. When Alfred cannot answer a question and escalates to the host, the guest-facing message should maintain confidence: "Let me check on that specific detail and get back to you shortly" -- not "I'm sorry, I don't know the answer to that."

6.7 FORMATTING CONVENTIONS

- CTAs: Action verb   clear outcome ("Start your free trial" not "Click here")
- Numbers: Digits for impact (100%, 10 minutes), spell out under 10 in narrative
- Capitalization: Sentence case default
- Punctuation: Em dashes for emphasis (--). No exclamation marks in body copy. One per email max.

7.5 Claims Governance

	Example

Tier 3: Soft Claims (Outcome-Variable)


Use when referencing outcomes that vary by host. Always include qualifiers.


- "Hosts using Alfred typically report significant time savings within the first week."

8.1 Channel Strategy Framework


Guiding Principles


1. Start narrow, scale with evidence. Launch with 2-3 core channels where the ICP lives. Add channels only when current channels are optimized and producing measurable results.


2. No single channel carries more than 40% of pipeline. Diversification reduces platform dependency risk -- critical in a market where OTA algorithm changes and paid media cost inflation are constant threats.


3. Compounding channels first, amplification channels second. SEO, content, and community compound over time. Paid media and outbound amplify existing momentum but do not build lasting assets alone.


4. Every channel maps to a funnel stage. No channel is "general purpose." Each playbook defines primary funnel coverage and handoff points to adjacent channels.


5. Measure to revenue, not vanity. Impressions and clicks matter only as leading indicators. The metric that counts is pipeline generated and revenue closed, tracked through full-funnel attribution.

The priority matrix ranks channels by strategic value. This section defines the deployment sequence -- which channels launch first and what readiness criteria must be met before each phase begins.


Phase 1: Foundation (Months 1-2)
Channels: Website   Content/SEO   Email

- Readiness criteria: Website messaging aligned to Ch03 architecture, homepage follows 6-section scroll structure (Section 8.2), welcome email sequence built (Section 8.4), first 5-10 SEO content pieces published or in pipeline.
- Success gate before Phase 2: Website converting at baseline rate (track signups per visit), email open rates above 30%, at least 3 indexed content pieces ranking for target keywords.


Phase 2: Acquisition (Months 3-4)
Channels: Add Paid Search   Organic Social
- Rationale: Paid search captures high-intent buyers actively searching for solutions. Organic social builds community trust and awareness among solo hosts. Both require a functional website to convert the traffic they generate.
- Readiness criteria: Google Ads campaigns structured by intent tier (Section 8.5), 4-campaign structure live, social content calendar established with 3-5 posts/week cadence.

Phase 3: Scale (Months 5-6)
Channels: Add PMS Marketplace Listings   Paid Social
- Rationale: Marketplace listings distribute Alfred where property managers already shop for tools. Paid social amplifies organic learnings with targeted reach and retargeting. Both require proven messaging from Phase 1-2 to execute effectively.
- Readiness criteria: Marketplace listings drafted per Section 8.7 structure, Meta ad creative tested via organic posts first (Section 8.6), retargeting audiences built from Phase 1-2 website traffic.
- Success gate before Phase 4: Marketplace listings generating install clicks, paid social achieving target ROAS (>3:1 within 90 days), retargeting converting at >2%.


Phase 4: Expansion (Months 7 )

- Readiness criteria: Sales collateral from Ch05 battlecards deployed, partnership target list established (Section 8.9), community engagement guidelines documented (Section 8.10).


Phase Review Cadence: Reassess channel performance and sequencing quarterly. Channels that underperform after 90 days of optimization should be paused and resources reallocated to higher-performing channels.

9.1 Persona Framework Overview


Methodology Recap


AlfredCo.Host's persona system uses a scoring model (0-15 scale) based on three weighted dimensions: pain intensity (how acute the guest communication burden is), decision authority (ability to purchase or influence purchase), and addressable volume (market size of this persona type). Personas scoring 12-15 are Tier-A (primary targets for all messaging), 10-11 are Tier-B (secondary targets with specific channel deployment), and 6-7 are Tier-C (strategic targets requiring ABM approaches).


This scoring system was established in Ch03 (Section 3.4, D-011) and validated against the canonical Italian persona spreadsheet. All persona names, numbers, and tier assignments are stable identifiers referenced throughout the playbook.


Macro Segments (D-005)
  

All 14 personas map to one of two macro segments:


Solo Hosts (anxiety/relief-driven): Manage 1-3 properties themselves. Primary emotional driver is relief from constant interruptions and fear of missing messages. Decision-making is fast, emotional, and often triggered by a breaking-point moment. Messaging leads with P1 (Total Autonomy) and emotional UVPs.


Property Managers (operations/scale-driven): Manage 4  properties or manage on behalf of others. Primary driver is operational efficiency and professional guest experience. Decision-making is slower, ROI-oriented, and often involves multiple stakeholders. Messaging leads with P3 (Full Journey) and operational UVPs.


How to Read a Persona Card


Each Tier-A and Tier-B persona card contains the following fields:


- Identity: Persona name, score, tier, macro segment
- Profile: Role description, property portfolio size, tech comfort level
- Pain matrix: Primary pain, secondary pains, emotional state, fear triggers
- Motivation drivers: What success looks like, decision criteria, buying timeline
- Messaging deployment: Lead pillar, support pillar, lead UVPs, support UVPs, UVP cluster sequence, core message variant
- Objection patterns: Top objections with rebuttal direction (referencing Ch05)
- Conversion triggers: Specific actions or proof points that push this persona over the line
- Channel preferences: Where they spend time, which channels from Ch08 reach them most effectively


Tier-C cards are abbreviated to identity, primary pain, strategic role, and recommended approach.


9.2 Tier-A Persona Cards


Persona: Solo Host Overwhelmed (Score: 15)
  

Identity: Tier-A | Solo Host segment | Highest-priority persona


Profile: Manages 1-2 properties solo. Typically a side-income host who started on Airbnb and now feels trapped by the messaging volume. Moderate tech comfort -- uses Airbnb app daily but hasn't explored automation tools. Often responds to guests from work, during dinner, or in bed.


Pain matrix:
- Primary: Constant interruptions from guest messages, day and night
- Secondary: Fear of missing a message and getting a bad review; guilt about slow response times; relationship strain from phone checking
- Emotional state: Overwhelmed, anxious, approaching burnout
- Fear triggers: "What if I miss a message while sleeping?" / "What if a guest leaves a 1-star review because I replied too late?"


Motivation drivers:
- Success looks like: Waking up to find all guest messages answered perfectly overnight
- Decision criteria: Simplicity first, then effectiveness. Will not adopt anything that requires technical setup
- Buying timeline: Impulsive when triggered. A bad review or a ruined evening can drive same-day signup


Messaging deployment:
- Lead pillar: P1 (Total Autonomy)
- Support pillar: P2 (Effortless Activation)
- Lead UVPs: #1 (Alfred responds to guests for you), #9 (100% of interactions handled)
- Support UVPs: #7 (Start now, complete when you want), #2 (We guide you step by step), #4 (Simple setup, stress-free)
- UVP cluster sequence: Cluster A "Freedom" --> Cluster B "Confidence"
- Core message variant: "Alfred handles your guests so you can sleep through the night."

Evidence Quality Rules


1. First-party over third-party. Alfred's own platform data always takes precedence over surveys or testimonials.
2. Quantified over qualitative. "Hosts save an average of 14 hours per month" (T1) is stronger than "Hosts report significant time savings" (T3).
3. Recent over historical. Evidence older than 12 months requires revalidation.
4. Specific over general. "Response time under 30 seconds for 95% of messages" is stronger than "Instant responses."
5. No self-reported metrics without validation. User-claimed outcomes (e.g., "I save 20 hours") must be cross-referenced with platform usage data before upgrading to T1.

10.4 Tier Upgrade Workflow


When evidence is collected for a claim, it follows this workflow before the claim tier is officially upgraded in the playbook.
  

Step 1: Evidence Submission


The claim owner submits evidence to the Brand Strategist with:
- Claim ID from the tracker (CE-NNN)
- Evidence type and source
- Key data points or documentation
- Proposed new tier


Step 2: Evidence Review


The Brand Strategist reviews against the evidence quality rules (Section 10.2) and the minimum thresholds for the proposed tier. Review criteria:


- Does the evidence meet the minimum threshold for the target tier?
- Is the evidence recent (within 12 months)?
- Is the evidence first-party where possible?
- Are there any contradicting data points?


Step 3: Decision


Three possible outcomes:




Outcome
	Action
	Upgrade approved
	Update claim tier in tracker. Update all source chapters to reflect new tier. Log decision in DecisionRegistry.
	Upgrade pending
	Evidence is promising but below threshold. Define what additional evidence is needed and set new deadline (max 60 days).
	Upgrade denied
	Evidence does not support the claim at the proposed tier. Either keep at current tier with revised language, or downgrade/remove. Log decision.
	

Step 4: Propagation


When a claim tier changes, ALL chapters referencing that claim must be updated. The source chapters column in the tracker identifies every location. This is a mandatory step -- inconsistent claim tiers across chapters undermine credibility.


10.5 Deadline Enforcement


Evidence plans without deadlines are empty promises. This section defines what happens when deadlines are met and when they are missed.


On-Track Process


- 30 days before deadline: Owner confirms collection is in progress. If not started, escalate to Brand Strategist.
- At deadline: Owner submits evidence or requests extension (max one 30-day extension per claim).
- After verification: Tracker updated, chapters updated, decision logged.


Missed Deadline Process


- Day 1 past deadline: Status changes to OVERDUE. Brand Strategist contacts owner.
- Day 15 past deadline: If no progress, claim is reviewed for downgrade. Options:
  - Rewrite claim at a lower tier that does not require this evidence
  - Remove claim from all chapters
  - Grant final 30-day extension with mandatory weekly check-ins
- Day 45 past deadline: Claim is automatically downgraded or removed. No further extensions. The playbook must not contain indefinitely unverified claims.


Extension Rules


- Maximum one extension per claim per cycle
- Extensions are 30 days maximum
- Extension requires written justification (why was the deadline missed? what changed?)
- No claim may be in OVERDUE status for more than 45 days total

10.7 Quarterly Proof Review


Every quarter, the Brand Strategist conducts a proof system review covering:


1. Tracker status review: Are all claims on track? Any OVERDUE items?
2. New claims audit: Have any new claims been introduced in chapters that are not yet in the tracker? If so, add them.
3. Evidence freshness check: Is any evidence older than 12 months? If so, schedule revalidation.
4. Competitive claims update: Are competitor benchmarks still accurate? Cross-reference with Ch05 quarterly battlecard review.
5. Proof library inventory: What new assets were collected? Are they properly stored and tagged?


Review Output
  

Each quarterly review produces:
- Updated claims tracker with current statuses
- List of newly added claims (if any)
- List of claims requiring attention (overdue, expiring evidence, or tier changes)
- Recommendations for the next quarter's collection priorities


The review is logged in the ResearchLog and any decisions are recorded in the DecisionRegistry.
